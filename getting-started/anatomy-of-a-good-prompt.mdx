---
title: "Anatomy of a good prompt" 
description: "The key elements that make a prompt give an AI agent the context and direction it needs."
---

A good prompt does not merely tell the model what to do. It gives the model enough information to make the right decisions while constraining the decisions you do not want it to make.
Realistically, not every prompt will include every single element outlined below, but these give you a mental framework for what a solid prompt should contain.


## 1. Objective

Define the actual outcome you want.

Tell the agent what the task is for: the purpose of the task, rather than merely what it should contain. This gives you a better output.

For example, if you simply tell an agent to give you a PRD for a feature, it will give you the PRD.

However, say:

> “Create a PRD that gives engineering enough information to estimate and build the feature, while helping design and product align on the user problem, scope, and acceptance criteria.”

The outcome is significantly better.

You can further improve the prompt by stating the sections you want included. Say you have an existing PRD documentation structure and you want the agent to follow it. You can include:

> “Create a PRD that gives engineering enough information to estimate and build the feature, while helping design and product align on the user problem, scope, and acceptance criteria.
>
> The structure should compulsorily have these sections: business objectives, user story, customer context, metrics to track, UI design, engineering, and edge cases.”

Needless to say, the output you’ll get using the third prompt outclasses the product of the first.


## 2. Context

Give the agent the information it needs to make good decisions, such as the problem at hand, the user, the business goals, existing market, product stage, stakeholders, and so on.


## 3. Decision criteria

Tell the model how to judge competing options.

A prompt has to tell the agent what to optimise for and what the permissible trade-offs are.

If you ask:

> “What should the onboarding flow look like?”

There are dozens of reasonable answers.

If you say:

> “Optimise for reducing time-to-value for a first-time user. Do not add steps unless they materially improve activation or prevent a meaningful failure.”

Now the model has a basis for making trade-offs.


## 4. Constraints

Setting constraints helps define technically possible but unwanted outputs.

Generally, based on my own usage of AI agents, I’d categorise constraints into three buckets:

- **Content constraints:** “Don’t introduce Y.”
- **Scope constraints:** “Only modify Y.”
- **Style constraints:** “Don’t use generic marketing language.”

Overall, it is important that your prompt has a change boundary.


## 5. Protect existing decisions

This is especially helpful when iterating on an already-provisioned output.

I have found that after several turns of conversation in the same chat, the agent forgets what’s been resolved or what was previously decided and starts changing things you already like. Hence, this is important.

State already established facts and decisions during refinement to avoid reopening decisions that have already been made.

A good framework here is to distinguish between what is **fixed** (things you are happy with and must remain unchanged), what is **flexible** (things the model may decide), and what is **open** (things you want the agent to recommend).


## 6. Specify outputs

Define what you actually want returned.

For example, if you are using an agent to find a KYC provider, instead of saying:

> “Give me providers who serve fintechs.”

A better instruction says:

> “Give me 3 KYC providers that provide BVN support for fintechs in the African market. For each, provide the recommendation, rationale, trade-off, and when I should choose it. End with your recommended option based on the context I have given you about my product/business. Return the output in Markdown so I can copy it or generate it in a table or document.”


## 7. Define your quality bar

Tell the agent what good sounds like.

This is different from decision criteria. Here, you are teaching the agent your taste rather than relying on its trained taste for your persona.


## 8. Reference material

Give the model the raw material it should reason from, such as a screenshot, website link, image, document, or anything else that gives the agent an idea of what you want the final output to look like.

This is especially important if you are working on a design task, image generation, or a prototype MVP.

More importantly, tell the model whether it should use the material as **evidence, inspiration, or authority**.


## 9. Ambiguity handling

When a prompt contains ambiguity, what should the model do?

Depending on the context of the task at hand, you can handle this in three ways:


### Ask

> “If a missing detail materially changes the recommendation, ask me before proceeding.”

### Assume

> “Make reasonable assumptions where necessary and state them briefly.”

### Branch

> “If there are two materially different interpretations, analyse both.”

This is particularly important for AI agents. Otherwise, the model may quietly invent assumptions and proceed as though they were facts.


## 10. Iteration instructions

In my experience, AI agents rarely land on the perfect answer/output in the first turn. You’ll likely have to tweak and modify your instructions in consecutive turns.

Here, you have to give your agent an error signal.

Instead of simply saying “make it better,” tell it what it did wrong in the prior turn, what to rewrite, and how to think differently to improve the next output. Should it check the internet for industry standards? Be specific.
